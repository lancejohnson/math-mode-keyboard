How reviews read the clock

The model behind non-speed reviews: what “expected time” means, how it falls with practice, how forgetting slows it, what each mastery level asks for, how we catch blown-off work, and how XP is awarded. Code: app/model/timing.js (tests in timing.test.js). Every chart below runs that same code.

0The short version

  • Every problem has a floor: the time someone who has truly mastered it would take — reading it, thinking through each step, and physically writing the answer.
  • Practice shrinks the gap above the floor. For one learner on one topic the gap shrinks roughly exponentially. Averaged over many learners it looks like the famous “power law of practice”.
  • Forgetting adds time back. A slow answer three weeks after the last review is partly fading memory, so we excuse that part before judging mastery.
  • We score an attempt by how much of the gap it closed (0 = beginner speed, 1 = at the floor), so every problem, learner and level sits on one 0–1 scale.
  • Time never fails a correct answer. It scales credit between 0.7× and 1.3× and decides whether the memory model hears “hard”, “good” or “easy”.
  • Mastery levels are points on the curve: recognize (no time rule), explain & sketch (starts promptly), fluent (within 1.5× the floor), automatic (within 1.2× the floor and consistent).
  • XP = minutes of focused work for a typical learner, with Math Academy’s carrot-and-stick bands, including a penalty for blowing off a task.

1The learning curve

T(n) = floor + (start − floor) · e−rate · n

n is the number of practices so far. start is a typical first attempt. rate is fitted for each learner on each topic from their own times. The dashed line is the power-law shape you get when you average many learners together; individual learners look exponential (Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort 2000).

Across: practices (n). Up: seconds.

Each simulated learner’s times wobble around the curve; the dots are re-scored with the same level rules the app will use. Notice that “automatic” needs the dots to bunch up, not just to fall. Dots can slip back a level when recent answers wobble; the stored record keeps the highest level shown, and a slip only shortens the next review interval.

2Where the floor comes from

floor = reading + thinking + writing
  • Reading: about 0.25 s per word of the prompt.
  • Thinking: about 1.35 s per solution step — the “mental preparation” step in the Keystroke-Level Model used to estimate expert task times (Card, Moran & Newell 1980).
  • Writing: number of symbols in the answer × this learner’s seconds per symbol. We measure that once with a short copy test (copy a printed expression), because handwriting speed differs a lot between people and is not math skill.

This is only the first guess. Once real attempts exist, the floor for a problem becomes the fastest 10% of correct times from experienced learners (or Lance’s own times), adjusted for each learner’s writing speed. If a learner keeps beating the floor, the floor was set too high and gets lowered.

3Forgetting slows recall

expected × (1 + k · (1 − R))

R is retrievability from the memory model: the chance the learner can still recall the topic today (1 right after a review, falling over the days). In the ACT-R memory model, recall gets slower as a memory weakens (Anderson; Pavlik & Anderson 2005). We start with k = 1, so at R = 0.7 we expect answers to take 30% longer, and we don’t count that 30% against mastery.

4Scoring one attempt

gap closed = (ln start − ln t) ÷ (ln start − ln floor), clipped to 0…1

Measured on a log scale, because halving your time from 40 s to 20 s is about as much progress as halving it from 20 s to 10 s. t is the answer time after the forgetting allowance.

Gap closedCredit multiplierGrade sent to the memory model
under 0.330.7× – 0.9×hard (next review sooner)

A wrong answer is “again” regardless of time.

Math Academy does the same in spirit: “if a student submits a correct answer but takes an excessively long time relative to the expected time for a student who has mastered the topic, the weight of that evidence is diminished” (mathacademy.com, How Our AI Works).

5Mastery levels as targets on the curve

Each topic carries a required level (default per topic, overridable per course or learner). A topic can have several requirements, each with its own memory record. A question type can only demonstrate levels up to its ceiling (multiple choice tops out at recognize).

LevelTime ruleWhy
RecognizeNone. Correct is enough.Recognition questions are quick by nature; time adds little.
Explain & sketchFirst stroke within 2× the start floor (reading + one step); at most 1 restart.Someone who understands starts promptly and doesn’t scrap their sketch twice. Total time varies too much with how much they choose to write.
FluentMedian of the last 3 correct answers within 1.5× the floor.Procedures you can do without struggling.
AutomaticMedian of the last 5 correct within 1.2× the floor, and spread of log times ≤ 0.25.Automatic skills are fast and consistent (Segalowitz & Segalowitz 1993). Usually earned on the speed track, not here.

Unlocking: a topic opens when every prerequisite has reached its required level. Partial credit from harder topics (FIRe) can keep a prerequisite’s memory fresh, but cannot raise its level — a level is only shown directly.

6Effort: Math Academy’s penalty, and whether we need one

What Math Academy does

“…in the absence of a penalty system, adversarial students will complete tasks that they feel are easy and then submit random guesses to intentionally fail out of tasks that require more effort. This tanks their performance, causing their adaptive learning schedule to slow down or even begin falling backwards.” — The Math Academy Way, p. 312
  • They call these students “XP hackers”: they are minimizing effort per XP. The penalty takes XP away when a task is blown off, “and even more XP if they continue blowing off tasks” (p. 313), so the cheapest way to earn XP becomes honest work.
  • Triggers are “patterns indicating lack of effort (e.g., rushing and guessing)” (p. 431); the FAQ says the system “detects that the student is rushing and guessing”.
  • Calibration: “how frustrated a teacher, tutor, or guardian sitting next to the student would become” (p. 313).
  • Result: many adversarial students’ pass rates rose from under 50% to over 90%; diligent students rarely see a penalty (p. 313).

Is it only because of multiple choice?

No. The book never ties the penalty to multiple choice. The problem is the incentive: failing a hard task gets you out of it (it is postponed and you are moved to other work), so a student who wants XP cheaply fails hard tasks on purpose. Multiple choice only makes the junk answer quicker to give. Free-response handwriting removes lucky guesses, not the incentive — a learner can still scribble “0” on every hard question to make it go away.

What we do

  • Detect, always. An answer is rushed if it is wrong and took less than half the time needed just to write the expected answer (we already know the writing floor). An answer is blank if no ink. Rushed and blank answers are not treated as forgetting — they tell the scheduler nothing about memory, so they don’t drag the schedule backwards.
  • Penalize only when there are stakes. For Lance learning on his own, there is no one to game, so the penalty can be off; the detection still protects the schedule. For students (children, classes), turn it on: two or more rushed answers making up half the task → negative XP (half the task’s XP, growing with repeat blow-offs, capped at 2×).
  • Explain & sketch questions are graded by features, so a junk sketch fails on content; the effort check catches the speed of giving up.

7XP: how Math Academy does it, and ours

Math Academy

  • 1 XP ≈ 1 minute of fully focused, productive work for an average serious (but imperfect) student (p. 309). A typical course is ~3,000 XP; prealgebra ~2,000, precalculus ~4,000, AP Calculus BC ~6,000 (pp. 49, 388).
  • Recommended pace: 20–40 XP a day, 3–5 days a week; 40 XP per weekday is the benchmark; at least 15 (FAQ; p. 389).
  • Every task type earns XP: lessons, reviews, multi-step problems and quizzes (a timed quiz roughly every 150 XP).
  • Carrot and stick (p. 311): bonus XP for perfect, full for nearly perfect, most for passable, a little for nearly passable, zero for poor, negative for blowing off a task.
  • XP is not progress. Progress is the share of topics completed and only moves when a lesson is passed; XP needed varies with performance (pp. 313–314).
  • Weekly leagues by XP, promotion and demotion (p. 310). Warning signs: failing more than 1 in 10 lessons, any negative XP, or half or fewer XP per minute worked (p. 431).
  • The exact numbers per band are not published.

Ours

  • Base XP = the typical learner’s expected minutes for the task, straight from the curve above (the typical learner’s n, not this learner’s). Faster learners therefore earn more XP per minute — which is the reward for real fluency.
  • Explain & sketch, sketch-the-graph and predict-observe-explain tasks earn XP like any other, by their expected time — so the intuition work that sets this course apart is not second-class.
  • Partial credit from other topics (FIRe) earns no XP; XP is only for work done.
  • Progress is shown separately: topics at their required level ÷ topics in the course.
